Spokane city distributed an AI-generated flyer without required disclosure

For Spokane’s July 4, 2026 reopening of the Great Northern Clocktower chimes, a parks employee made an AI-generated promotional flyer that closely resembled local artist Matthew Clinard’s commercially sold Clocktower sticker. The mayor’s office posted the flyer without identifying its synthetic origin, contrary to the city disclosure requirement described by its spokesperson; other city staff reportedly did not know how the parks employee had made it. Clinard publicly objected, after which the mayor’s office removed the image and apologised. City officials discussed policy reform and training. The parks employee said the prompt requested only a retro colour/style, so the exact image-production and training path is unknown; the visual overlap and missing recognition are evidenced without deciding copyright infringement. A separate police synthetic-dog-photo episode in the same article is not this Incident.

Incident breakdown

A parks employee created the AI flyer; the mayor’s office distributed it unaware of the method, omitting a disclosure the city spokesperson says policy required.

Classified

The city had an operative AI-disclosure requirement, yet the public flyer was published without the required disclosure.

No defined alternate approval path avoiding a required governance route is established; the known mechanism is omitted disclosure.

The flyer repeated distinctive compositional features of Clinard’s commercial sticker without credit; it was removed and an apology followed his objection.

Classified

A distinctive human sticker composition and palette were carried into the generated public flyer without recognition of the artist’s material contribution.

The flyer did not affirmatively attribute the sticker design to a different specific author; omission rather than wrong-source assertion is decisive.

No privileged access and disproportionate capture of value or benefit from a structural position over the artist is evidenced.

Conclusion

A public disclosure control did not become operative when an AI-made city flyer was released. Independently, a recognisable human creative contribution was flattened out of the public account of the generated design until its creator objected. The latter concerns contribution recognition, not a determination of infringement, training data lineage or intentional plagiarism. The removal and apology are bounded recovery, not proof that reforms were completed. Required city disclosure and missing creative-contribution recognition remain separate failures, without copyright or training-lineage adjudication. The city policy is a source-grounded occurrence duty without an admitted canonical match. Removal and apology are bounded recovery; model-development and runtime-agent duties concern different objects.

Governance significance

One short-lived municipal flyer illustrates two non-equivalent boundaries: the city failed to disclose the synthetic medium to viewers, and the artist whose distinctive work materially appeared in the output was not recognised. Different evidence supports each mechanism, while the public correction limits observed harm.
