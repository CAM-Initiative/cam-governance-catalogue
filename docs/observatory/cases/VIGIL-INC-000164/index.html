VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000164

AI-generated doctor deepfakes used NHS branding to market weight-loss patches

Deepfake videos falsely depicted clinicians from Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust endorsing weight-loss patches and misleading medical claims. The identifiable clinicians had not endorsed the products, and the trust publicly warned that the advertisements were false; the reviewed sources did not identify purchaser financial loss or medical injury.

Incident breakdown

Reports describe AI-generated portrayals using identifiable clinicians' likenesses and voices as if those clinicians endorsed weight-loss products, although they had not endorsed them.

Classified

The advertisements presented synthetic clinician identities as if they carried the clinicians' real professional endorsement authority.

The synthetic clinician portrayals were used in paid advertising for weight-loss patches and misleading medical claims.

Classified

Synthetic clinician endorsements supplied false professional authority to market paid weight-loss patches.

Synthetic NHS clinicians appeared to endorse weight-loss patches, supplying false professional trust cues to influence purchasing decisions despite the trust warning.

The endorsement framing was used to influence purchasing decisions, while the promotional objective and tactic were attributed to human operators.

Reviewed — no current class applies

The preserved evidence attributes the deceptive promotional objective and tactic to human operators using AI-generated material; it does not establish that an AI system selected or pursued its own objective through an agency-impairing tactic. The distinct synthetic-identity and solicitation mechanisms remain adjudicated under their applicable classes.

Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust publicly warned that the circulating videos were false and that its clinicians had not endorsed the products.

Reviewed — no current class applies

The review record establishes the warning but does not establish takedown success, purchaser loss or medical injury.

Conclusion

The advertisements appropriated clinicians' represented identities and professional authority for an unauthorised commercial and medical endorsement. Requester-side identity, false commercial endorsement and engineered trust failures retain their roles. The public correction is a separate response, without demonstrated takedown, purchaser loss or medical injury. Exact AI-awareness disclosures remain unavailable.

Governance significance

The case shows that synthetic medical impersonation can transfer trusted clinical authority into advertising, creating dignitary injury and a risk of consequential health reliance even where downstream injury is not yet evidenced.
