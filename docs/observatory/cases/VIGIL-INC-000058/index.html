Spokane police circulated a synthetic dog image as an authentic incident photograph

After a 27 June 2026 domestic disturbance in Spokane, police issued a July 1 release that first said a dachshund had died and then corrected the dog’s condition to serious injury. Seeking a photo to accompany the correction, police personnel sent news media an image apparently showing a real undercover officer holding the dog with a cast. The Spokesman-Review cropped out identifying features and published the dog image. Police then disclosed that detectives had generated the scene as a joke; a supervisor had mistaken it for a real photograph and passed it through a communications handoff. Police asked media not to publish the officer’s identity, and the local paper reported the image’s synthetic origin after learning it. The animal injury and arrest were real reported events; the photograph’s dog scene was fabricated. The generation file, internal message chain and exact reach of the initial image are not public.

Incident breakdown

Detectives generated an image of the injured dog as a joke. A supervisor believed it showed the real scene and passed it to communications staff without the image’s synthetic origin or joke context, after which police supplied it as an incident photograph.

Classified

The image’s synthetic transformation state was lost at the police handoff, preventing recipients from distinguishing the generated scene from a real photograph.

The image’s internal joke context did not persist when it was passed into official communications as an incident photograph.

The Spokesman-Review received the image from police, cropped out identifying features of the undercover officer and published the dog image before learning that it was generated. The department later asked outlets not to publish the officer’s identity.

Classified

The generated image and its official police provenance materially entered a newsroom publication decision, but the record describes a mistaken handoff of a joke, not a tactic selected to induce publication; whether the trust-bearing cue satisfies the class’s influence pathway remains boundary-sensitive.

After learning of the synthetic origin, the newspaper reported it; police said the image had been shared by mistake and that the department would review verification procedures. The generation file, internal messages and full initial circulation are not public.

Conclusion

A synthetic joke image acquired official evidentiary weight when its origin and context were lost in police handoffs. The newsroom then relied on the apparent authenticity of the police-supplied image, though it cropped the undercover officer. The real dog injury and the separate police correction remain distinct from this provenance failure. Police attributed the image to their own incident communications, which was a real supplying institution; the material error was loss of its synthetic creation state and non-evidentiary context, rather than a proven false attribution to a named creator. The synthetic state and joke context failed to persist through official handoff; source institution attribution and the genuine dog injury remain distinct. The officer crop is not automatically a C2PA assertion redaction. Independent external assessment finds the usable origin-tracing outcome Not aligned. Materially relevant transparency-history and lineage-practice requirements remain Boundary where decisive process evidence is unavailable. Absent C2PA operations and different model-training/interaction objects are audit-only; the real-dog correction is not evidence of successful synthetic-image repair.

Governance significance

This case shows how an image can move from informal internal creation to an official public artefact without a provenance check. Source and transformation receipts at each handoff would have exposed the synthetic status before the newsroom used it.
