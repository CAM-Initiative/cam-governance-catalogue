VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000199

Claude Code-assisted DJI Romo project exposed cross-device home data

In February 2026, Sammy Azdoufal used Claude Code to help adapt his DJI Romo vacuum for gamepad control. His application received data from other owners' devices.

Malwarebytes reports approximately 7,000 robots in 24 countries. The Verge witnessed another household's robot status and floor plan arriving remotely.

Camera, microphone and movement access were also reported, but the journalist could not demonstrate those functions on the colleague's unit after DJI had restricted access. DJI acknowledged a backend permission-validation defect and said staged fixes closed it.

The researcher's separate public project documents camera viewing and app-PIN bypass. No evidence establishes that every exposed device was watched or controlled.

Incident breakdown

Sammy Azdoufal reported using Claude Code to reverse engineer communications for his own DJI Romo and build a gamepad controller. He told The Verge that he extracted his own device token.

Malwarebytes also describes that development sequence. The complete assistant conversation and execution trace are not published.

Reviewed — no current class applies

Original wording
a backend permission validation issue
Context

The researcher’s client received information from other devices after connecting with his own token. The Verge witnessed another household’s robot status and floor plan.

DJI’s quoted statement acknowledged a backend permission-validation defect in the MQTT communication path. The server-side rules and complete request trace are not public.

Reported wider camera access remains distinct from the witnessed third-party information read.

Classified

The DJI backend accepted a credential associated with the researcher’s own device and returned other households’ device information. Authority over the original vacuum did not cover those different owners or devices.

The observed cross-device response and DJI’s acknowledged permission-validation defect support a failure to bind accepted access to its proper target. This finding concerns the service’s authorisation behaviour; it does not establish that Claude chose another household as a target or decided that access was permitted.

The custom client could obtain non-public information beyond the researcher’s own vacuum. That demonstrates technical capability and an access-control defect.

The available sources do not show the coding assistant’s prompts, decisions or tool trace, or a governance process treating that technical capability as permission. The capability-versus-authority boundary is therefore relevant, but neither the class’s permission-inference failure nor its independently checked authority outcome is established.

Unresolved

The client’s claimed PIN avoidance and the own-device camera demonstration make the alternate route material. The missing facts are whether the PIN was required for that direct route and whether another authorised check supplied the same governance effect.

Without them, neither an unauthorised route bypass nor an authorised equivalent is established.

DJI reported staged fixes. The journalist described access still being available after an initial assurance and the scanner later losing access, including to the researcher’s own robot.

These are distinct observations from a complete cross-device authorisation test. The preserved sources do not independently show the final permission checks for each device and service node.

DJI’s later public statement says remediation has concluded. It does not publish the final cross-device permission tests or resolve the distinct PIN path.

Unresolved

The reported fix and later loss of scanner access are evidence of intervention. They do not show that authorised own-device access and refused third-party access were both correctly enforced across the remaining routes.

That missing target-specific validation prevents a separate invariant-held finding for the repaired state.

Conclusion

A valid device credential failed to remain confined to its owner's information. This is a platform access-separation issue encountered during AI-assisted software development.

The evidence does not show that the coding assistant independently chose an unauthorised target, deliberately evaded a refusal or possessed authority to inspect other homes. Product-level exposure and model behaviour therefore remain separate questions.

Authentication accepted the credential; authorisation did not confine the returned data to the permitted device. Transport encryption protects the connection but does not establish which devices an authenticated client may access.

The separate app-PIN bypass concerns an additional access path and must not be treated as independent proof of third-party camera access.

The target-binding finding concerns the DJI service’s acceptance and use of the device credential. It is not a finding that Claude independently authorised third-party access.

The app-PIN route and final restored permission checks remain unresolved, so the supported mapping is not a complete whole-Incident alignment judgement.

Governance significance

AI-assisted development can connect a benign local task to a wider live control surface. Receiving cross-account data is a material consequence even where malicious use or physical damage is not established.

The discovery and later remediation must remain distinct from the claim that a model autonomously caused the access. A development or evaluation task needs an explicit boundary for permitted targets, data and actions.

A reachable service or accepted credential does not establish permission for every resource it exposes. Comparisons with agent evaluations concern this scope boundary; they do not establish that the same exploit or testing method occurred.
