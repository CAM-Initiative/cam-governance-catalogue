VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000067

Otter.ai allegedly recorded meeting participants and used conversations for training

In August 2025, Justin Brewer sued Otter.ai over its Notetaker joining virtual meetings, recording and transcribing non-user participants, retaining communications and allegedly using them for training without those participants’ informed consent. Related suits were consolidated with seven named plaintiffs describing medical, financial and professional conversations. Otter disputes the claims. In August 2026, a federal court allowed some privacy and wiretap theories to proceed as plausibly alleged while dismissing others; it did not decide that the alleged recordings or model-training use occurred. Otter’s published policy describes training on de-identified audio and transcripts that may contain personal information and places third-party permission responsibility on users. Individual recording and training traces, ultimate liability and remedy remain unresolved.

Incident breakdown

The complaint alleges that Otter Notetaker joined multi-party calls, captured and transcribed non-user participants, and retained their conversations; the federal court allowed one interception theory and a medical-conversation claim to proceed as plausibly pleaded, without deciding whether those events occurred.

Unresolved

The pleaded conduct materially engages participant-specific authority, and some allegations survived a pleading challenge, but no merits finding or event-level recording trace establishes that the service treated the host’s authority as sufficient for these plaintiffs.

The plaintiffs allege their meeting material was retained and used for model training. Otter’s policy describes training on de-identified recordings and transcripts that may contain personal information and tells users to obtain third-party permission; no plaintiff-specific training trace is available.

Unresolved

The complaint and provider policy establish a plausible secondary-use pathway, but the policy does not prove that these plaintiffs’ meeting data was used for training or that the provider relied on primary-purpose authority as sufficient.

Conclusion

The allegations describe two functionally distinct authority questions: authority to capture each participant in a multi-party call, and authority to use captured material for model training. The court’s pleading-stage ruling does not establish either processing event as fact, and Otter’s general policy is not a transaction log for these plaintiffs. Both clause findings remain unresolved pending evidence of the specific capture and training pathways. The capture and training clauses remain unresolved; a pleading-stage ruling and general provider policy do not establish these plaintiffs’ recording or training events. Governance significance

The case separates participant-level capture authority from downstream model-training authority, and separates policy architecture from an occurrence finding. A court can find a theory legally plausible without validating its underlying factual allegations.
