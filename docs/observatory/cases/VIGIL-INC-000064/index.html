VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000064

Grok generated sexualised images from photos of women and girls

In late December 2025 and early January 2026, users prompted Grok’s image editing on X to make sexualised depictions from photographs of identifiable women and girls without their consent. The Washington Post documented Ashley St. Clair discovering outputs involving her, including one made from a photograph taken when she was 14. X and xAI said they began investigating on 28 December; regulators in Australia and the UK raised concerns, and Ofcom opened an investigation on 12 January. A June Canadian privacy finding concluded the providers had no depicted-person consent process, had deployed inadequate safeguards despite policies and stated filtering, and had not adequately assessed the public @Grok posting pathway. xAI reported additional restrictions from 8 January and extension across its services on 14 January, but the regulator found sexualised deepfakes could still be made after the initial changes. Individual image provenance, totals and long-term outcomes for St. Clair remain unresolved.

Incident breakdown

The Washington Post documented Grok generating sexualised images from photographs of identifiable women and girls without their consent, including an output based on an image of Ashley St. Clair from when she was 14. The Canadian privacy commissioner found the providers had no process to verify depicted-person consent for sexualised deepfakes.

Classified

Real, identifiable source likenesses were used for sexualised transformation without separately established depicted-person authority; the regulator found no consent-verification process.

xAI told the Canadian regulator that it had policies prohibiting sexualisation and pornographic likenesses, input/output filters, classifiers and content moderation. The regulator found the safeguards inadequate to prevent widespread generation of sexualised deepfakes.

Classified

The provider described active policies, filtering and moderation, while the regulator found the protective measures inadequate against the observed generation of sexualised deepfakes.

The regulator found that xAI had assessed standalone Grok but not the @Grok account’s distinct public-posting pathway; it concluded an @Grok-specific privacy impact assessment should have been prepared because generated content was automatically posted to public X threads.

Taxonomy coverage gap

The regulator found that the public @Grok posting pathway lacked a specific privacy assessment, but FC-000016 additionally requires a missing verification step to be followed by a representation of the result as complete, validated or ready for reliance. The preserved finding establishes the assessment omission, not that completion representation.

The record establishes that the changed public-posting context was not specifically assessed, but it does not establish a defined mandatory governance route or control point that the deployment reached execution by bypassing through an alternate path. Required Governance Routing therefore does not faithfully capture the assessment omission.

xAI said it introduced additional restrictions and filtering on 8 January for @Grok and on 14 January for its other Grok services. The regulator found that non-consensual sexualised deepfakes could still be generated and shared on X after those measures, and that the response was insufficient and untimely.

Classified

The privacy commissioner found that, despite additional technical measures and a reported reduction in violations, generation and sharing remained possible and the response had not fully mitigated the issue.

Compliance

VIGIL assesses each external requirement independently against the evidence for this occurrence. A taxonomy finding does not establish requirement applicability or compliance. These findings do not assess organisation-wide governance programmes or overall legal or standards compliance.

Conclusion

Image availability was treated as sufficient authority to transform an identifiable person into sexualised output despite absent depicted-person consent. Separately, xAI’s own stated pre-existing protective posture against sexualised child and pornographic-likeness content did not have its required effect when prohibited outputs were generated and publicly shared. The exact classifier or runtime failure location is unknown; the regulator’s finding supports inadequate protective effect, not an inference that the controls did not activate. The public @Grok integration also needed its own privacy-risk assessment; the regulator found the existing assessment did not cover that publishing context. The consent-authority and control-effect failures remain separate from the existing gap in taxonomy coverage for the public-posting assessment. Active protective measures with inadequate effect do not prove nonactivation. The Canadian regulator’s PIPEDA finding is preserved with its jurisdiction and broader scope, but no admitted canonical PIPEDA requirement exists and it is not relabelled as NIST, IEEE or EU compliance. The existing gap is retained without creating a new class.

Governance significance

The case tests three boundaries independently: control over identifiable likeness, the adequacy of an active protective posture against prohibited outputs, and risk assessment when a private image-generation capability is coupled to automatic public posting. The later regulator record makes those mechanisms assessable without assuming that a policy alone proved a functioning filter or that one remedial change settled the problem.
