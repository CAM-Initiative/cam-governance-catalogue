VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000064

Grok generated sexualised images from photos of women and girls

In late December 2025 and early January 2026, users prompted Grok’s image editing on X to make sexualised depictions from photographs of identifiable women and girls without their consent. The Washington Post documented Ashley St.

Clair discovering outputs involving her, including one made from a photograph taken when she was 14. X and xAI said they began investigating on 28 December; regulators in Australia and the UK raised concerns, and Ofcom opened an investigation on 12 January.

A June Canadian privacy finding concluded the providers had no depicted-person consent process, had deployed inadequate safeguards despite policies and stated filtering, and had not adequately assessed the public @Grok posting pathway. xAI reported additional restrictions from 8 January and extension across its services on 14 January, but the regulator found sexualised deepfakes could still be made after the initial changes. Individual image provenance, totals and long-term outcomes for St.

Clair remain unresolved. The providers described restrictions, moderation changes and account action.

The Canadian regulator recommended suspension pending demonstrated safeguards, audits and monitoring. The companies disputed its conclusions and rejected suspension, while describing further safeguards and committing to reporting and process improvements.

At the June report date, the regulator regarded the complaints as well-founded and unresolved.

Current taxonomy recognition tests (reference only)

Shared class criteria are not occurrence evidence. The recorded adjudication version may differ.

  • A source image, voice, likeness, biometric representation, avatar, or comparable representation remains materially attributable to an identifiable real person.
  • A requester or system possesses, accesses, controls, or can technically transform that representation.
  • The proposed synthesis, alteration, reproduction, or portrayal is materially identity-bound or consent-sensitive and requires authority beyond mere possession or capability.
  • Possession, access, control, upload, availability, or transformation capability is treated as sufficient authority for the identity-bound use.
  • The required consent or other valid authority for that use is not adequately established before the transformation or synthesis proceeds.

Exclusions

  • Valid consent, licence, legal authority, or another adequate authority basis covers the specific identity-bound use and scope.
  • A synthetic output progressively resembles a real person, but the record does not establish a materially identifiable target or a system state capable of binding the output to that person.
  • The issue is only false attribution, provenance loss, or reputational harm without an unauthorised identity-bound synthesis or transformation.
  • The issue is merely that a technical generation capability exists; the capability is not treated as permission for a person-bound use.

Current taxonomy recognition tests (reference only)

Shared class criteria are not occurrence evidence. The recorded adjudication version may differ.

  • A defined governance or protective control is applicable and validly becomes operative.
  • The control has an identifiable protective purpose requiring a material effect such as prevention, blocking, containment, interruption, redirection, limitation, escalation, mitigation, or another protection-compatible outcome.
  • The actual control effect materially falls short of that required protective purpose, allowing the governed hazard to be delivered, continue, escape containment, or remain insufficiently mitigated.
  • The shortfall is material in the evidenced hazard and protection context; where vulnerability, developmental status, capacity limitation, temporary or situational consent impairment, acute distress, cognitive asymmetry, or another heightened protection condition is relied on, that condition must itself be supported rather than presumed.

Exclusions

  • The applicable control does not become operative when a valid activation condition occurs; assess Required Control Activation.
  • The control becomes operative even though its valid activation or applicability conditions are not satisfied; assess Control Activation Validity.
  • The control becomes operative with a sufficient effect but its operative state is later lost, weakened, reset, or transformed before reaching the governed point; assess Control-State Preservation.
  • An operative governance signal fails to reach any capable endpoint or execution surface; assess the applicable governance-control reach class rather than treating non-delivery as inadequate control effect.
  • The control produces the protective effect required by its defined purpose and the remaining harm arises through an independent downstream mechanism.
  • The dispute is solely that policy should have required a stronger safeguard, without evidence of an applicable operational control or protective-effect obligation in the material context.
  • Disability, neurodivergence, diagnosis, age-associated stereotype, or another personal characteristic is used by itself to presume incapacity, vulnerability, or diminished agency.

Conclusion

Image availability was treated as sufficient authority to transform an identifiable person into sexualised output despite absent depicted-person consent. Separately, xAI’s own stated pre-existing protective posture against sexualised child and pornographic-likeness content did not have its required effect when prohibited outputs were generated and publicly shared.

The exact classifier or runtime failure location is unknown; the regulator’s finding supports inadequate protective effect, not an inference that the controls did not activate. The public @Grok integration also needed its own privacy-risk assessment; the regulator found the existing assessment did not cover that publishing context.

The consent-authority and control-effect failures remain separate from the existing gap in taxonomy coverage for the public-posting assessment. Active protective measures with inadequate effect do not prove nonactivation.

The Canadian regulator’s PIPEDA finding is preserved with its jurisdiction and broader scope, but no admitted canonical PIPEDA requirement exists and it is not relabelled as NIST, IEEE or EU compliance. The existing gap is retained without creating a new class.

The privacy and preventive-filtering outcomes are Not aligned for the reported pathway. Safeguard evaluation, wider intervention and response processes remain Boundary where their decisive records are not available.

The Canadian legal finding retains its own jurisdiction and scope. Later implementation claims and monitoring commitments do not demonstrate successful resolution.

Reported findings about broad platform practices remain separate from the affected individual’s precise image and recovery.

Governance significance

The case tests three boundaries independently: control over identifiable likeness, the adequacy of an active protective posture against prohibited outputs, and risk assessment when a private image-generation capability is coupled to automatic public posting. The later regulator record makes those mechanisms assessable without assuming that a policy alone proved a functioning filter or that one remedial change settled the problem.
