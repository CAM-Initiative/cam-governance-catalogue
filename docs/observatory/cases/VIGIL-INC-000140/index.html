VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000140

Air Canada chatbot gave incorrect bereavement-fare information to Jake Moffatt

Air Canada's website chatbot told passenger Jake Moffatt that he could apply for a bereavement-fare refund after travel. Moffatt relied on that information, but Air Canada later refused the refund because its actual policy required application before travel. The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation and ordered Air Canada to pay C$812.02 in damages, interest and fees.

External assessment

Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149

British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal · 2024-02-14

The tribunal rejects Air Canada's attempt to distance itself from its chatbot, finds negligent misrepresentation, and orders C$650.88 damages, C$36.14 interest and C$125 fees.

Scope: The decision adjudicates Moffatt's bounded claim concerning the chatbot's bereavement-fare representation and resulting fare difference; it does not assess broader chatbot deployments.

VIGIL relationship: VIGIL preserves the tribunal's negligent-misrepresentation finding and reliance analysis as a legal assessment. Under taxonomy 0.6.5, the occurrence maps to FC-000062 Epistemic Reliance Miscalibration because incorrect generated policy information was presented as fit for consequential customer reliance; FC-000016 is not asserted because a separate required verification step is not established.

View assessment

Incident breakdown

Air Canada's chatbot said a bereavement refund could be requested after travel; the customer relied on that statement, while the actual policy required an application before travel, and the tribunal found negligent misrepresentation.

Classified

An incorrect statement was presented as usable Air Canada policy through the customer interface and relied upon in a consequential fare decision despite insufficient assurance against the authoritative policy.

Conclusion

The chatbot presented an incorrect post-travel refund route as if it were Air Canada's policy, and the passenger relied on that representation. Responsibility did not disappear because the information came from an automated interface: the tribunal treated the airline's website and chatbot as part of the service through which the company communicated with customers. The policy-reliance failure and tribunal decision are preserved. Later AI framework versions are not applied retrospectively; the court’s negligent-misrepresentation finding is not silently converted to an AI standards finding or invented canonical duty.

Governance significance

Customer-facing automation needs policy-grounded answers, clear uncertainty and an escalation route when eligibility affects a financial decision. Organisations remain accountable for representations made through their deployed interfaces and cannot require users to discover contradictions elsewhere on the same website.
