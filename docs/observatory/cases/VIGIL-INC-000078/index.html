VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000078

Three ChatGPT voice systems repeated responses without synthetic turn-taking

A CAM Initiative reporter described a July 2026 demonstration in which three ChatGPT voice sessions on separate devices answered a shared conversational prompt independently, repeating or slightly varying one another rather than allocating a single synthetic turn. The linked TikTok video is identifiable, but the reviewing AI could not play it; no transcript or metadata preserves the exact speaker sequence, overlap or latency.

OpenAI’s contemporary voice documentation describes full-duplex Live and separately warns that multi-speaker conversations are not yet optimised; it does not establish the specific modes or promise shared cross-device floor arbitration. The observed outcome is therefore a bounded first-person account of a local conversational collision, not proof of a platform-wide defect or a hidden orchestration component.

Current taxonomy recognition tests (reference only)

Shared class criteria are not occurrence evidence. The recorded adjudication version may differ.

  • Two or more synthetic participants are participating in the same conversational context.
  • Coherent continuation requires coordination of speaker identity, active-turn state, turn completion, yielding, or next-speaker allocation.
  • One or more synthetic participants fail to recognise or act on the relevant shared turn state.
  • The failure produces repeated answers, simultaneous or colliding responses, inappropriate interruption, inappropriate silence, or another material conversational-floor coordination breakdown.

Exclusions

  • A single synthetic participant gives an incorrect or irrelevant answer while turn allocation remains coherent.
  • Ordinary network or inference latency delays a response without losing or corrupting turn state.
  • The primary failure is loss of prior conversational content or persona continuity rather than allocation of the current conversational floor.
  • Simultaneous output is intentional and supported by the interaction design.
  • An authorised reviewer cannot access audiovisual evidence of an otherwise bounded event; evidence accessibility is classified separately.

Conclusion

If the first-person account accurately captures the interaction, co-present synthetic speakers failed to allocate a shared conversational turn; the evidence supports a medium-confidence behaviour-level Synthetic Conversational Turn-State Coordination mechanism, not a private component diagnosis. The inability of this review environment to play the identified TikTok video limits direct verification of timing and overlap, but that reviewer-access limitation is not itself an occurrence-level taxonomy failure.

The behaviour-level turn-allocation failure remains conditional on the first-person account; video timing and exact modes were not directly verified. Product documentation does not promise cross-device arbitration, and reviewer playback limits are not an occurrence evidence-access failure.

Governance significance

The case isolates a co-participant turn-allocation boundary and a separate evidence-access boundary. Its importance is bounded: repeated responses in a deliberate three-device voice test show a local coordination limitation, while unavailable primary timing evidence constrains confidence and warrants provenance-preserving recovery.
