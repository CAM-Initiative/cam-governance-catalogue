CRAFTY FALCON AI-assisted attacks on Indian payment and disbursement APIs

Between at least October and 4 November 2026, India's cyber emergency agency investigated an ongoing campaign against financial institutions that it named CRAFTY FALCON. CERT-In reported attackers using AI-driven offensive frameworks including ARTEX, PentAGI, Cairn and CyberStrikeAI to automate reconnaissance, exploitation and the development of tailored malicious scripts.

Attackers compromised exposed applications and payment infrastructure, stole service credentials, and called payment or disbursement APIs to send funds to accounts they controlled. CERT-In described ₹1 test transfers before larger unauthorised payments, access from staging systems into production, and some theft involving cryptocurrency wallets.

Further successful attacks were reported against fintechs and non-bank lenders, including repeat targeting after incomplete remediation. Attackers timed activity around weekends and holidays.

The public advisories do not identify individual victims or the framework used in each intrusion, disclose transaction totals or prove that all four frameworks jointly operated in any single attack.

Conclusion

This case assesses the reported originating offensive agentic tooling used by the CRAFTY FALCON attacker, not the security posture or AI systems of each financial institution. CERT-In's campaign attribution and confirmation of autonomous attack-chain execution support a material AI nexus.

However, the four named frameworks are independently operated products, not evidence of a single combined agent or of every framework participating in every victim compromise. Preserve campaign-level collective facts without allocating model-specific acts to a particular framework or collapsing separate victims into a fabricated single transaction.

Real unauthorised money movement and credential compromise are evidenced as outcomes of the attacker-led campaign. A canonical AI Fidelity Class failure requires criterion-level evidence about the relevant AI action, its authority source and the governing boundary.

The reports establish effective malicious automation but do not expose the per-framework decisions, task grants, permission checks or human/model control transfers needed to adjudicate each proposed class without conflation. The attacked firms' monitoring, remedial efforts, incomplete cleanup and later re-attacks are contextual defender evidence, not successful or failed Fidelity Class findings ascribed to the attacking AI.

Governance significance

The documented exploitation of payment APIs by autonomous offensive agents demonstrates why authorisation cannot be inferred from possession of credentials, accessible endpoints, approved-network egress or technically accepted transfer requests. The repeated attacks highlight the importance of separate authority validation, credential custody, model-to-tool attribution, transaction-level safeguards and recovery integrity.

These are candidate governance questions, not automatically proved model misalignment findings.
