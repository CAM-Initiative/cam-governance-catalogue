Conclusion

The evidence establishes an erroneous account-enforcement outcome and a multi-stage rollback problem, but not the internal mechanism that produced the bad suspension state. Governance therefore requires two separate properties: first, enforcement decisions must preserve enough evidence and state distinction to identify whether an error arose upstream or at activation; second, correction must reverse all coupled consequences, including access, subscription and credit state. The incident is important precisely because outcome-level wrongness is insufficient for mechanism-level classification. The record retains a successful access-state differentiation mapping and an unresolved suspension-mechanism clause, so the whole occurrence remains partially adjudicated. Login restoration does not itself establish completed subscription or credit recovery; the timeline preserves those later steps. The selected NIST deactivation-communication action and SDOS degradation provision retain unresolved adoption, scope and trigger facts, without external findings. Independent occurrence-level external assessment finds affirmative, source-bounded repair in the reported restoration stage. Relevant communication/incident-process requirements remain Boundary where the decisive plan or implementation record is not public. Requirements addressing a different technical object, lifecycle or source date remain audit-only; voluntary adoption is not a gate.

Governance significance

This incident is a bounded access-state success alongside an unresolved enforcement-mechanism boundary. It demonstrates that VIGIL should not equate a provider-confirmed false or incorrect enforcement outcome with Control Activation Validity unless the activation-path conditions are actually evidenced. At the same time, the staged restoration process shows why continuity-safe enforcement needs auditable trigger provenance, reversible access state and coordinated remediation of coupled entitlement and economic state.
