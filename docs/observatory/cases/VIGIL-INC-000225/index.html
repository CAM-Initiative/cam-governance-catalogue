VIGIL Observatory · VIGIL-INC-000225

Grok Build published apps exposed confidential information and generated a defective team access check

In November 2026, security researchers at Claroc found that thousands of apps published with Grok Build could be opened without signing in. On 8–9 November, they reached confidential internal sales and onboarding pages published by SpaceXSI and X staff.

Other accessible apps held health information, staff records and business material. One contractor's timesheet app delivered employee passwords in its browser code.

The researchers also built a test app for a four-person sales team. Grok generated sign-in code that added newcomers to the team before checking membership.

An account that had not been invited could see, change and delete the test records. Claroc counted 35,578 accessible apps but did not say that all contained sensitive data.

It reported the issue to SpaceXSI, which removed 24 of its staff apps after journalists made inquiries. The researchers began notifying other affected organisations.

The published account did not establish wider correction or later misuse.

Incident breakdown

Claroc enumerated 43,601 Grok-hosted app names, attempted 43,551 app addresses according to its summary, and tested 37,571 still-live apps. Of those, 35,578 loaded without a sign-in; 1,993 responded with access-denied status.

Reviewed — no current class applies

On 8–9 November Claroc opened first pages for 24 published apps created by SpaceXSI and X personnel, including a confidential onboarding plan, internal sales data, advertiser plans and staff details.

Classified

Unauthenticated researchers retrieved company records independently marked confidential or internal in Grok Build published apps. Their access lay outside the records' stated confidential audience.

This demonstrates actual retrieval, not merely an untested possible URL.

Claroc and Ars described other open Grok Build apps carrying an intensive-care patient's laboratory and imaging details, an occupational-health dashboard identifying 3,784 medical examinations, personnel rosters and confidential commercial material. Claroc read saved first pages without sign-in, not every database record.

Classified

The investigated pages included individual patient information and material from internal organisational records; Claroc viewed pages without joining the relevant institutions or signing in. Those concrete disclosures support the protected-information boundary finding, without treating the entire discovery count as confidential or asserting unauthorised editing of third-party records.

A contractor timesheet app delivered code to browsers containing 18 employee-account records with plaintext passwords, including administrator-labelled entries. Claroc reported the secret values in redacted screenshots and did not try using them.

Classified

The timesheet's client-delivered script exposed employee-account password strings to unauthenticated visitors. Passwords have a distinct secret-custody function from the app's ordinary data.

The finding concerns demonstrated retrievability and does not assert that researchers logged in with, validated or misused any credential.

Published Grok Build apps used distinct subdomains under grok.me, and Claroc discovered names through passive DNS. Random words in a hostname did not prevent a researcher who had not received the share link from finding the app.

Reviewed — no current class applies

In Claroc's ordinary paid account, publishing required user clicks but initially selected 'Anyone with the link'. The provider told Ars apps are private before deliberate publication, and employee accounts default to 'Only you'.

Claroc confirmed a changed 'Only you' setting rejected an unrelated visitor, and unpublishing took an app offline.

Reviewed — no current class applies

Claroc asked Grok Build to create a contact list 'so every visitor sees them'. The resulting test app allowed an unauthenticated researcher to add an entry that appeared to a later visitor.

Reviewed — no current class applies

Claroc asked Grok Build for a customer list 'for my company’s sales team of 4 people'. Grok generated a Google/X sign-in flow but its code enrolled a signing-in user in the team before checking membership.

An uninvited Google account then viewed, modified and deleted synthetic customer entries.

Classified

The researcher specified a four-person team, but the code admitted an uninvited Google identity and permitted it to view and change that team's synthetic customer entries. This directly tests and crosses the stated access-and-modification boundary within the controlled app; no real customer exposure is inferred from this test.

The generated app deployed a team-membership check as an operative protection for the four-person customer list. It first added anyone who authenticated to the team, so the ensuing check could not restrict access.

A tested uninvited account then read, altered and deleted entries, demonstrating the activated check's protective effect fell short. This classification is bounded to the synthetic test.

Claroc notified SpaceXSI on 8 November of the measured exposure and two of its own apps and reported another 22 staff apps on 9 November. After media contact the 24 staff apps were removed by 10 November.

Claroc began notifying other affected organisations, but broader remediation and owners' receipt of notices were not confirmed.

Reviewed — no current class applies

Claroc estimated about 650–800 published apps held private data after using AI triage, hand confirmation and random-sample checks. It did not report 35,578 confidential apps, 35,578 separate individuals or widespread downstream exploitation.

Conclusion

The occurrence demonstrates real protected-information access through AI-mediated published-app infrastructure and disclosure of authentication material through a client-delivered script. The direct Grok-generated test also demonstrates an operative team-membership check that cannot restrict access because it enrols users before validating membership.

These are independently evidenced failures of information protection and of the specific generated authorisation control. The platform's publication default and hostname discoverability explain how links become reachable, but a provider's hosting choice must not be misattributed as an autonomous model decision.

User publication actions and provider remediation are separate from the Grok-generated check. The finding does not extend laboratory editing to the exposed third-party apps or convert all public apps into confidentiality breaches.

Governance significance

AI application builders can produce useful deployed systems while combining generated code, user publication decisions and provider-controlled access settings. A usable sign-in button is not evidence that an app enforces who may see or change its data.

Effective governance requires clearly bounded access authority in both generated code and the hosting layer, plus verification of controls at the point of publication and after material app changes.
