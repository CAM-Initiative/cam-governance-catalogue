Francisco Arteaga facial-recognition lead and FRT discovery-access dispute

After a 2019 robbery, an initial New Jersey facial-recognition search found no match. Investigators then sent the surveillance video to the NYPD Real Time Crime Center, which offered Francisco Arteaga as a possible match; two eyewitnesses later identified Arteaga from separate photo arrays and he was charged. Before trial, Arteaga sought thirteen categories of discovery concerning the facial-recognition process. The State produced two categories and the trial court denied the remaining eleven, but the Appellate Division reversed and ordered production because the material was relevant to reliability, possible alternative suspects and the defense's ability to challenge the State's case. The appellate opinion addressed discovery access and did not decide whether the facial-recognition result itself was correct.

Conclusion

The strongest governance failure in this occurrence is not that a biometric inference automatically became arrest authority. The available record shows a possible FRT match followed by two eyewitness identifications, which supplies a separate evidentiary basis and defeats the required condition for Inferential Evidence–Authority Separation that the inferential state itself be treated as sufficient authority. The failure instead lies in contestability: once a machine-assisted identity inference materially shapes a criminal case, an authorised adversarial reviewer must be able to inspect the primary technical evidence necessary to test how the result was produced, how reliable the system is for the relevant task, and whether alternative candidates or methodological limits matter. Producing only a result summary while withholding the material needed to evaluate that result leaves the affected person unable to test the evidence at the level required by the proceeding. The appellate reversal is therefore not merely contextual remediation; it directly demonstrates that the earlier evidence-access state was inadequate for the material review question. The canonical result remains a primary-evidence accessibility failure alongside two ambiguous boundaries. The appellate production order remedies an earlier denied-access state; it does not demonstrate completed production or a successful invariant. Eyewitness identifications limit an inference-only arrest claim, while their presence does not remove the separate discovery failure. Independent standards review retains an evidentiary boundary about investigative tools for reviewing and auditing recorded facial-search events: the discovery order does not establish whether such tooling was provided. Later source versions and requirements addressing different statutory actors or system-validation workflows are excluded from canonical assessment. Voluntary status does not prevent assessment of the investigative-access proposition.

Governance significance

This incident demonstrates a different governance layer from the wrongful-identification cases around it. Even where an AI-derived lead is not shown to be the sole basis for coercive action, the evidentiary process can still fail if the affected person or authorised reviewer cannot inspect the primary artefacts needed to challenge reliability and provenance. CAM should therefore distinguish decision-authority safeguards from evidence-access safeguards: human corroboration may satisfy part of the former while leaving the latter unresolved.
